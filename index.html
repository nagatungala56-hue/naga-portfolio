Artifact 01 · AI Lab

Study Path — An AI Workshop Planning Assistant

Design Thinking Lab (Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test) · Built on the Claude API

Introduction

Study Path is an AI assistant I designed and prototyped that takes a course's syllabus or list of due dates and breaks each weekly "workshop" into a day-by-day task plan, so students can see exactly what to work on and when instead of facing one large deadline at the end of the week.

Description

Online, asynchronous courses give students freedom over their schedule, but many only realize how much a workshop requires a day or two before it's due — by then, discussion forums are close to closing and there's no time for revision. I ran this problem through a full design-thinking cycle: empathizing with the root cause (lack of visibility, not lack of ability), defining the problem statement, and ideating three possible solutions before building and testing the one that actually solved it.

Objective

To design and prototype an AI assistant that reduces the planning burden on busy online students by converting a syllabus into a concrete, dated task breakdown — rather than just another reminder tool.

Process

Compared three concepts (a manual calendar template, a browser reminder extension, and an AI chat assistant) and selected the AI assistant since it does the planning work rather than requiring the student to do it first. Built and tested it against this course's own six-workshop schedule, then iterated on the system prompt twice based on real output quality.

Tools & Technologies Used
Claude (Anthropic) Claude API Prompt Engineering Design Thinking Framework
Testing & Iteration

The first version of the assistant produced generic output ("work on workshop" repeated across days). I revised the system prompt to require small, action-specific tasks (e.g. "draft discussion post," "respond to two peers," "review rubric before submission"), which measurably improved usability. A classmate who tested it separately found it especially useful for a two-week-long workshop, where pacing is easy to lose track of.

References

Anthropic. (2025). Claude API documentation. https://docs.claude.com  |  AIML-500 course syllabus and workshop schedule (used as the test dataset for the assistant)  |  Design Thinking framework (Empathize–Define–Ideate–Prototype–Test), Stanford d.school.

Value Proposition

This shows I can take a vague AI output and diagnose why it's vague — not just re-prompt randomly, but identify the specific missing constraint (task granularity, personalization to study availability) and fix it deliberately. That's a directly transferable skill to production analytics work, where model or dashboard output is only as good as the specification behind it.

Unique Value & Relevance

Most AI-assistant demos stop at "it works." This one documents the failure mode first (generic, unhelpful plans) and the specific prompt-engineering fix that resolved it — the kind of before/after evidence that matters to an employer evaluating whether I can ship reliable AI-driven tools, not just call an API.

Artifact 02 · Pitch Sprint

Before the Rush — A Predictive Staffing Tool for Emergency Rooms

AIML-500 Residency, Pitch Sprint · Supervised Learning / Time-Series Forecasting

Introduction

Before the Rush is a pitch deck I authored for a proposed AI tool that forecasts ER patient volume shift-by-shift, so hospitals can staff for a surge before it hits instead of scrambling once the waiting room is already full.

Description

ER shifts are staffed to a fixed schedule, but patient volume isn't fixed — a bad flu week or a cold snap can double the waiting room overnight with no added staff. I designed a supervised, time-series regression model concept trained on years of hourly visit logs, weather forecasts, regional illness surveillance data, and local event calendars, refreshed daily as new data comes in. The deck walks a funder through the problem, the one-sentence solution, how the model would learn, its key limitation, and a scoped, low-risk pilot ask.

Objective

To translate a real operational pain point (reactive ER staffing) into a scoped, fundable AI pilot — practicing how to pitch a technical ML concept to a non-technical, business-facing audience.

Process

Followed a problem → solution → mechanism → risk → ask structure. Defined the model as a supervised time-series regression problem, identified the three data sources that would actually predict a surge, then deliberately included the model's honest failure mode (unprecedented events look like nothing in its training history) before asking for a single-ER, single-flu-season pilot rather than a full rollout.

Tools & Technologies Used
Time-Series Regression Supervised Learning Design Data Storytelling Slide Deck Design
References

AIML-500 Residency, Pitch Sprint assignment materials  |  Public hospital ER volume and seasonal illness surveillance trends used to motivate the problem (CDC FluView, ILINet)  |  Course readings on supervised time-series forecasting methods.

Artifact 03 · AI Coaching Session

Reasoning Through Real ML Failure Modes with an AI Coach

Machine Learning Training Methods Coach (AI Chatbot) · Applied Problem-Solving Across Three Data Scenarios

Introduction

This artifact is a worked transcript from a guided AI chatbot activity in which I reasoned through three progressively harder, real-world data problems: missing values, extreme class imbalance, and data drift. Rather than being handed answers, I had to propose and defend my own approach to each scenario before receiving feedback.

Description

The coach presented three progressively harder, production-style data challenges: a subscription churn dataset with 30% of income values and 15% of time-spent values missing, a fraud detection dataset with a severe 0.5% positive class and asymmetric misclassification costs ($5,000 per missed fraud vs. $150 per false alarm), and a deployed credit risk model whose calibration had drifted after both a debt-to-income shift in the applicant population and an undisclosed change to how income was verified upstream. For each, I proposed and defended a full plan before receiving feedback, then had to reconcile that feedback with my original reasoning rather than just accepting it.

Objective

To practice diagnosing and reasoning through realistic data-quality and model-reliability problems — not just naming the right technique, but explaining why it applies here specifically and what it trades off against alternatives.

Process

For missing data, I used median (not mean) imputation for the skewed income field, proposed KNN imputation using correlated features, and added a missingness flag rather than dropping 30% of records — then had to reconsider whether that flag risked leakage before concluding it was valid signal, not leakage, since missingness would exist at inference time too. For the fraud problem, I translated the $5,000-to-$150 cost asymmetry into a class-weighting ratio, argued for threshold tuning against the actual cost matrix instead of the default 0.5 cutoff, and proposed anomaly detection as a complementary safety net for fraud patterns unseen in training — then revised my own approach after feedback that a raw 33:1 weight can over-correct and should be tuned rather than plugged in directly. For the credit risk model, I separated data drift (population change) from concept drift (a changed relationship between features and outcomes), correctly flagged the new income-verification method as a likely pipeline/schema issue rather than organic drift, and proposed recalibration before a full retrain since the model's ranking (AUC) was still intact.

Tools & Technologies Used
AI-Guided Case Reasoning Imputation Strategy (Median, KNN) Cost-Sensitive Classification & Threshold Tuning Data Drift vs. Concept Drift Diagnosis
Testing & Iteration

Each scenario pushed back on my first answer rather than simply confirming it — I had to justify scaling features before KNN imputation, reconsider whether a missingness flag was leakage, and recognize that a cost ratio isn't automatically the right model weight without validation. On the final scenario, feedback prompted me to separate a genuine population shift from what was really a silent schema change in a re-verified income field, and to recognize that AUC staying stable doesn't mean calibration is fine — a distinction I hadn't drawn on my own initial pass.

References

AIML-500 Machine Learning Training Methods Coach (AI chatbot activity), course platform  |  Course materials on imputation strategy, cost-sensitive classification, and data drift vs. concept drift.

Value Proposition

This shows I can reason about data quality and model reliability problems the way they actually show up in production — not as isolated textbook exercises, but as judgment calls with trade-offs, where the "right" fix depends on why the data looks the way it does.

Unique Value & Relevance

Artifacts 1 and 2 show me building and pitching AI systems. This artifact shows the diagnostic judgment underneath both: recognizing what's actually wrong with a dataset or a deployed model, and choosing a fix based on the mechanism of the problem rather than a default playbook — a skill directly relevant to maintaining models in production, not just shipping them once.

Artifact 04 · Case Study Analysis

From Lab to Clinic — A Case Study on Deep Learning for Diabetic Retinopathy Detection

AIML-500 Case-Study Exploration · Model Validation, Ethics, and Post-Deployment Monitoring

Introduction

This artifact is a critical analysis of one of the most thoroughly documented end-to-end ML projects in healthcare: Google's deep-learning system for detecting diabetic retinopathy (DR). I traced it across two paired studies — the original model-development paper (Gulshan et al., 2016) and a follow-up human-centered field study of the same system after deployment in Thai clinics (Beede et al., 2020) — to evaluate the full lifecycle from data preparation through post-deployment reality.

Description

Gulshan et al. trained a CNN (Inception-v3, pretrained then fine-tuned) on 128,175 retinal images, each graded 3–7 times by licensed ophthalmologists, and validated it on two independent held-out clinical sets (EyePACS-1, Messidor-2), reaching AUCs of 0.991 and 0.990. On paper, this is a model that works. Beede et al. then observed the same system in eleven real clinics and found that inconsistent exam-room lighting, unreliable connectivity for image uploads, and variation in how patients' eyes were photographed degraded image quality and performance in ways the lab-based AUC never anticipated — not a flaw in the model's architecture, but a mismatch between validation conditions and deployment conditions.

Objective

To evaluate a real, peer-reviewed ML system against the full lifecycle the course emphasizes — data preparation, model selection, validation, ethics, and monitoring — rather than judging it on accuracy alone, and to identify what I'd carry forward or avoid in my own future work.

Process

Assessed the data-cleaning and labeling protocol (majority-vote grading, inter-grader agreement tracking) and the rationale for transfer learning over training from scratch. Flagged where the study was strong (independent held-out test sets, a clinically meaningful metric, sensitivity/specificity trade-off analysis across operating points) versus thin (no reported performance breakdown by demographic subgroup, camera type, or region; no documented consent, de-identification, or access-control plan for the clinical images used). Then connected the Beede field results back to a concrete recommendation: monitoring for input-data drift, subgroup and site-level auditing, and a front-line feedback channel should be designed into the validation protocol before deployment, not reconstructed afterward as a separate study.

Tools & Technologies Used
Model Validation Analysis Sensitivity/Specificity Trade-off Evaluation AI Ethics & Bias Review Post-Deployment Monitoring Design

(2016). Development and validation of a deep learning algorithm for detection of diabetic retinopathy in retinal fundus photographs. JAMA, 316(22), 2402–2410. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.17216  |  Beede, E., Baylor, E., Hersch, F., Iurchenko, A., Wilcox, L., Ruamviboonsuk, P., & Vardoulakis, L. M. (2020). Proceedings of the 2020 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems, 1–12. https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376718

This shows I can evaluate an ML system the way it needs to be evaluated in high-stakes fields — not just "does the AUC look good," but whether the validation population matches the deployment population, whether fairness and privacy were actually documented, and whether a monitoring plan exists before real patients depend on the output.
